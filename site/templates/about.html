Names used on this site

For the Rāmāyaṇa, Baroda Text (ಬರೋಡಾ ಪರಿಷ್ಕೃತ ಪಾಠ) is the Baroda Critical Edition. Traditional Text (ಪರಂಪರಾಗತ ಪಾಠ) is the vulgate, the longer text handed down with the commentaries. Either can be switched off in Options. The Kannada names are proposals and have not been reviewed by a Kannada scholar.

For the Mahābhārata, Traditional Text (ಪರಂಪರಾಗತ ಪಾಠ) is the Kumbakonam edition, a southern-recension print of 1906–10, and Pune Text (ಪುಣೆ ಪರಿಷ್ಕೃತ ಪಾಠ) is the critical text reconstructed at Pune.

The Bhagavadgītā uses the same two names: its Traditional Text is the Kumbakonam Mahābhārata and its Pune Text is the Pune critical text.

The Bhagavadgītā

The Bhagavadgītā has no separate source of its own here: it is cut out of the two Mahābhārata texts above. It is Bhīṣma Parva adhyāyas 25–42 in the Kumbakonam edition and 23–40 in the Pune text, extracted by etl/extract_gita.py into 700 verses in each edition. Each verse links back to its place in the Mahābhārata, and the Bhīṣma Parva adhyāyas that hold the Gītā link to the Gītā section.

Notes from the extraction:

  • The Kumbakonam text has an extra verse at the start of adhyāya 13 (13.1, prakṛtiṃ puruṣaṃ caiva…) and prints 13.25–26 as one record, so its adhyāya 13 runs to 35 verses; verse numbers are those of the print.
  • Speaker lines (arjuna uvāca, śrībhagavān uvāca and the like) are shown above the verse they introduce, not as verses of their own.
  • The GRETIL file adds two things to the Pune text that are not Mahābhārata text, at 6.23.1 and at the end of 18.78 (a Gītā recitation preface and an appendix). They were removed; see etl/fix_mbh_gita_preface.py. The raw preface is kept in data/gita/gretil_preface.txt.
  • There is no word-by-word analysis for the Gītā yet, because the Digital Corpus of Sanskrit leaves these adhyāyas out. The verses have no word panel.

Gītā Dhyāna

Shown as adhyāya 0, before the first adhyāya, are the nine verses traditionally recited before the Gītā, in homage to the Gītā, Vyāsa and Kṛṣṇa. They are traditionally ascribed to Madhusūdana Sarasvatī, but the authorship is uncertain. They are not part of the Mahābhārata. We typed the text ourselves, checking it word by word across several web copies and not copying any one; where the copies differ, the readings are recorded in data/gita/dhyana.json. The copies used:

The two editions and how they are linked

The Critical Edition is the shorter, reconstructed text; the Vulgate is the longer text most traditional readers and pārāyaṇa books follow. Every Vulgate verse is linked to its Critical Edition counterpart where one exists, and the reverse.

Linking is automatic. Both texts are reduced to a phonetic key that ignores vowel length, aspiration, retroflexion, sibilant differences and word breaks, which matters because this Vulgate text often writes sandhi undone. Verses are compared by shared three-letter sequences, sargas are paired by majority, matches must keep their order within a sarga, and verses that don't match whole are compared line by line. Uncertain links are listed for review in data/crosswalk/review.tsv.

On a Vulgate sarga page you can hide verses that are not in the Critical Edition; on a Critical Edition page, the same switch hides the few verses not found in the Vulgate.

About the Vulgate text

The Vulgate text comes from Sanskrit Wikisource and is noisier than GRETIL's. Mechanical repairs were applied and counted: a colon typed for visarga, a final ह् typed for visarga, leftover ITRANS fragments such as श्R^ङ्ग, and entries holding two numbered verses, which were split. Verses that still contain stray characters carry a note. Sandhi is left as the source has it. Because undone sandhi changes the syllable count, metre is shown for a Vulgate verse only when it scans cleanly.

How the text is shown

Each verse is converted once, at build time, from the source transliteration into Kannada script, Devanāgarī and IAST. The conversion is letter-for-letter and reversible; nothing is retyped or corrected by hand.

Kannada and Devanāgarī follow print convention: a word ending in a consonant is written joined to the next word (ವಾಲ್ಮೀಕಿರ್ಮುನಿಪುಂಗವಮ್), and the avagraha joins the word before it. IAST keeps the source's word breaks.

The Options menu can show homorganic nasals (ಪುಙ್ಗವ) instead of the anusvāra (ಪುಂಗವ), as many Karnataka pārāyaṇa books print them. Only the display changes.

Metre

Metres are identified automatically from syllable weight: a syllable is heavy when its vowel is long, when it ends in anusvāra or visarga, or when two or more consonants follow. About 95% of the Critical Edition is Anuṣṭubh. Verses that fit no listed pattern show no metre rather than a guess. The Chandas index in the Pariśiṣṭa (appendix) lists every metre found, with counts for each kāṇḍa and parva of each text and verses to read. The metre names, gaṇa formulas and descriptions there are written for this project from standard Sanskrit prosody and have not yet been checked by a reader of Sanskrit metrics.

Search

Search runs in your browser on a phonetic key. Long and short vowels, aspirated and plain stops, retroflex and dental letters, and the three sibilants are each treated as the same, and spaces are ignored so a word is found even when sandhi has joined it to its neighbour.

Word by word

In the Baroda text of the Rāmāyaṇa and the Pune text of the Mahābhārata, each verse can be opened to show its words with their grammar. The analysis comes from Oliver Hellwig's Digital Corpus of Sanskrit (CC BY 4.0), whose verse numbers follow those two critical editions. Compounds are shown as one word with its members joined by hyphens. Verses that match a verse of the critical text in the Traditional (Vulgate or Kumbakonam) text show that verse's analysis instead of repeating it; if the readings differ a little, the panel says so.

Gaps: in the Rāmāyaṇa, 48 verses of the Baroda text have no analysis. In the Mahābhārata, 1,110 of the 73,821 Pune verses have none, chiefly the Bhagavadgītā (Bhīṣma Parva 23–40), which the corpus leaves out, one adhyāya of the Droṇa Parva (7.173) and about 300 scattered verses. Those verses have a dimmed icon. In the Kumbakonam text, only verses that match a Pune verse exactly, or nearly, have word-by-word meanings.

The meanings shown are dictionary senses of each base word (lemma), not a translation of the word in that verse. They were chosen automatically from Apte's Practical Sanskrit-English Dictionary (1890; copyright © 2014 The Sanskrit Library and Thomas Malten, Cologne Digital Sanskrit Lexicon, CC BY-NC-SA 3.0), except for about 600 frequent words (pronouns, particles, common verbs, and the names and epithets of both epics) whose English glosses were written for this project because the first dictionary sense misleads: Apte's first sense for arjuna is "silvery", for karṇa "having long ears". For rarer words the automatic choice can be wrong. About 94% of word occurrences in the two epics have a meaning.

Kannada meanings are given for about 500 words: the names, epithets, pronouns, particles and commonest small words of both epics (together nearly half of all word occurrences). The panel shows them first when the script is Kannada, with the English underneath. They are written for this project by Claude and have not yet been reviewed by a Kannada reader, so please treat them as a convenience and report mistakes. For other words, tap the word in the panel to see its entry in the Dictionary (below); the panel itself shows only the English meaning. The Kannada grammar terms (case names, tenses) have not yet been reviewed by a Kannada grammarian either; corrections are welcome.

Dictionary

The Dictionary button in the bottom bar opens the Śabdārtha Kaustubha, a Sanskrit–Kannada dictionary of about 46,800 headwords compiled by Chakravarti Srinivasa Gopalacharya, as a small app of its own (Vedāvalokanam, the same project that has the Vedas; a copy of it is built into this site, so it also works offline once opened). In the word-by-word panel, tapping a word shows its dictionary entry under it: Kannada meanings, derivation and a link to the full entry. Words are matched by sound, so spelling variants such as पुत्र and पुत्त्र find each other; about 83% of the word occurrences in the two epics have an entry, and the rest (some pronoun stems and verb roots among them) show nearby headwords instead.

Licence: the digital text comes from the indic-dict project, whose repository states no licence. It is shown here on the decision of this site's owner, as in the Vedāvalokanam app; if the rights holder objects it will be removed. Typing slips corrected in the source are listed in the Vedāvalokanam repository.

Known source issues

The GRETIL file has a few structural irregularities, kept as they are: 595 verses have three lines, 84 have one, five have four, and two line identifiers are repeated (for example at 5.15.24). These are listed in data/normalized/ce_report.json in the repository.
